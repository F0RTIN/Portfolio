The Warmth of
Analog Matter
Tangible phenomena, embodied affordances, and the creative agency of physical matter — an interactive CRT installation as a site of encounter between human hands and nonhuman vitality.
01 — Premise
What Digital Cannot Simulate
Analog technologies do not represent the world — they transduce it. A vinyl groove is a physical trace of acoustic pressure; a CRT phosphor is excited by actual electrons. This directness produces warmth: a quality born of material imperfection, drift, and noise.

02 — Affordance Theory
The Body Knows
Gibson's concept of affordance names the action-possibilities a thing offers a body. Analog objects afford physically: a potentiometer invites the grip, the turn, the resistance. Its feedback is continuous — no quantization, no menu. The body knows what to do before the mind interprets.
03 — The Installation
Cathode Variations
A vintage CRT monitor plays an analog video loop — signal routed through original circuitry, never digitized. The internal electronics have been physically modified: potentiometers soldered directly onto deflection yoke circuits, color guns, and sync generators.

Each knob reaches into the electron beam itself. Turning a control does not send a command to software — it alters voltage directly, bending the physics of cathode emission and magnetic deflection in real time.

This is not a simulation of analog warmth. It is analog process, made tangible and interactive. The visitor becomes a co-author of the image — negotiating with electromagnetic fields through their hands.
04 — Vital Materiality
When Matter Organizes Itself
Jane Bennett's vibrant matter challenges the border between inert stuff and lively agency. The CRT electron beam is not a symbol of vitality — it is a form of it. Following Kafka's Odradek — animate wood, spontaneous structure — the tube resides in a world where the line between inert matter and vital energy is permeable.
"A hint of the animate in plants, and of the vegetable in animals."
— Gilles Deleuze, Bergsonism, 1991
05 — Research Questions
What We Investigate
RQ1  Does direct manipulation of physical circuits produce qualitatively different felt experience than software-mediated control?

RQ2  Can material imperfection — noise, drift, bloom — be designed as aesthetic resource rather than engineering flaw?

RQ3  How does Bennett's "vibrant matter" framework reframe the CRT as co-author rather than tool?
Manuel De Landa describes the power of nonhuman materiality to "self-organize": inorganic matter-energy has a wider range of alternatives for the generation of structure than just simple phase transitions. Even the humblest forms of matter have the potential for self-organization — coherent waves called solitons form in ocean waters and lasers alike. Stable attractors sustain coherent cyclic activity. When put together, these forms of spontaneous structural generation suggest that inorganic matter is much more variable and creative than we ever imagined. This insight into matter's inherent creativity needs to be fully incorporated into our new materialist philosophies.
— Manuel De Landa, via Jane Bennett · "The Force of Things: Steps toward an Ecology of Matter" · Political Theory, June 2004
